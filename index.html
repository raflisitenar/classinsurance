Insurance in 60 seconds
Insurance is a contract for sharing specified financial risks.

You pay a premium for defined coverage. If a covered event occurs, the insurer evaluates the claim according to the contract, including its limits, deductibles, conditions and exclusions.

Coverage Library

Common types of insurance

Different policies address different risks. The examples below are general descriptions, not promises of coverage. Always read the actual policy wording.

01

Auto Insurance

May address liability to others, damage to an insured vehicle, medical expenses and other eligible driving-related losses. Requirements and available coverages vary by location.

02

Home Insurance

Can help protect a dwelling, personal property and personal liability against specified events. Flood, earthquake and other risks may require separate or additional coverage.

03

Health Insurance

Helps pay eligible healthcare expenses according to plan rules, provider networks, deductibles, copayments, coinsurance and other cost-sharing provisions.

04

Life Insurance

Can provide a death benefit to named beneficiaries when policy requirements are met. Common structures include term coverage and forms of permanent life insurance.

05

Travel Insurance

May cover specified cancellations, interruptions, delays, emergency medical costs or baggage losses. Covered reasons and exclusions are especially important to review.

06

Business Insurance

A broad category that may address property, liability, employees and operational risks. Needs vary substantially by industry, size and business activity.

Fundamentals

How insurance works

Although policies differ, the basic relationship usually follows a similar pattern.

01 / Choose

Identify the risks you want to transfer and select a policy with appropriate terms.

02 / Premium

Pay the required premium to establish and maintain coverage.

03 / Event

A loss or event occurs that may fall within the policy's coverage.

04 / Claim

Notify the insurer and provide the documentation or information it requests.

05 / Review

The insurer evaluates the facts, policy terms and any amount that may be payable.

Practical Guide

How to read an insurance policy more carefully

Insurance contracts can be dense, but a structured review makes them easier to understand.

1. Start with the declarations or schedule

Check the named insured, policy period, covered property or persons, stated limits, deductibles and endorsements. Incorrect basic information can create problems later.

2. Find the insuring agreement

This section generally describes the broad promise being made by the insurer. Read it together with definitions because ordinary words can have specific contractual meanings.

3. Identify exclusions and exceptions

An exclusion removes certain risks from coverage. Some exclusions contain exceptions that restore limited coverage, so reading only the heading may not be enough.

4. Review limits and sublimits

A headline policy limit may not apply equally to every category. Certain property, services or events can have smaller sublimits.

5. Understand your duties

Policies can require timely notice, reasonable steps to prevent additional damage, cooperation with an investigation or specific documentation.

6. Read endorsements

Endorsements can add, remove or modify terms in the main policy. The final contract should be read as a complete package.

Pricing Basics

What can affect an insurance premium?

Pricing methods differ by insurer, product and jurisdiction. Depending on the type of insurance, permitted factors may include the amount of coverage, deductible, characteristics of the insured risk, location, claims history, selected features and other underwriting information.

Coverage amount

More protection can increase the insurer's potential obligation. Comparing prices only makes sense when the coverage being compared is reasonably equivalent.

Deductible

A higher deductible can shift more of an eligible loss to the policyholder. It may affect premium, but it also increases the amount you may need to fund yourself.

Risk characteristics

Insurers evaluate characteristics associated with the risk being insured. The relevant factors differ greatly between auto, property, health, life and commercial products.

Policy options

Optional coverages, endorsements and higher limits can change both the scope of protection and the price. Compare the final contract, not just the headline quote.

Simple example

Policy A costs less than Policy B, but Policy A has a substantially higher deductible and lower limit. Calling Policy A “better” based only on price would ignore the different amount of risk retained by the policyholder.

Claims Guide

What happens after a loss?

Exact procedures vary, but understanding the general claim process can make policy language easier to interpret.

Notify the insurer

Policies commonly require notice after a potentially covered event. Follow the insurer's stated reporting process and keep relevant confirmation or reference numbers.

Document the event

Depending on the circumstances, useful records may include photographs, receipts, inventories, reports or other evidence. Do not create or alter documentation.

Coverage review

The insurer may investigate what happened and compare the facts with definitions, coverage grants, exclusions, limits, deductibles and conditions in the policy.

Resolution

A claim may result in payment, partial payment, a request for more information or a denial based on policy terms. If something is unclear, ask for an explanation tied to the contract.

Common Mistakes

Insurance decisions that deserve a second look

Choosing only by price

A lower premium is not automatically equivalent coverage. Check limits, deductibles, exclusions and optional features before comparing the final cost.

Ignoring exclusions

Knowing what is not covered can be as important as knowing what is covered. Pay particular attention to risks that are realistic for your situation.

Assuming limits are unlimited

Policies generally cap payments. Some categories can also have separate sublimits that are lower than the overall policy limit.

Failing to update information

Changes in property, use, household circumstances or business activity can affect insurance needs. Ask the insurer whether material changes should be reported.

Reference

Insurance glossary

Premium

The amount charged for insurance coverage.

Deductible

An amount the policyholder may be responsible for under specified terms before applicable benefits.

Coverage Limit

The maximum amount payable under specified policy terms.

Sublimit

A smaller limit that applies to a particular category within broader coverage.

Exclusion

A circumstance, cause or type of loss the contract does not cover.

Claim

A request for coverage or payment under an insurance policy.

Beneficiary

A person or entity designated to receive certain policy benefits.

Underwriting

The process of evaluating risk and determining whether and on what terms coverage may be offered.

Policyholder

The person or entity that owns an insurance policy.

Liability

Legal responsibility for injury, damage or another covered obligation.

Endorsement

A document that changes, adds or removes terms from a policy.

Policy Period

The dates during which the policy is scheduled to provide coverage, subject to its terms.

Insured

A person or entity that qualifies for coverage under the policy's definitions and terms.

Peril

A cause of loss, such as fire or theft, depending on the context of the policy.

Replacement Cost

A valuation approach that may relate to the cost of replacing damaged property, subject to policy terms.

Common Questions

Insurance FAQ

Is cheaper insurance always better?

No. A lower premium may come with different deductibles, limits, exclusions or features. Compare equivalent policy terms rather than price alone.

Does insurance cover every loss?

No. Coverage depends on the policy contract and circumstances of the loss. Policies contain definitions, conditions, limits and exclusions.

What is the difference between a premium and a deductible?

A premium is the price charged for insurance coverage. A deductible is an amount the policyholder may be responsible for in connection with a covered loss, depending on the contract.

Why do premiums vary between people or properties?

Pricing can reflect the coverage selected and underwriting factors permitted for that product and jurisdiction. The relevant factors differ by insurance type.

What is an exclusion?

An exclusion identifies circumstances or losses that are outside the policy's coverage. Exclusions can contain exceptions, so the complete wording matters.

What is a coverage limit?

A coverage limit is a maximum amount payable under specified policy terms. Policies may also contain sublimits for particular categories.

Should coverage be reviewed periodically?

Periodic review can help identify whether changes in property, finances, household circumstances or other relevant factors affect your coverage needs.

What should I read before purchasing?

Review coverage, limits, deductibles, exclusions, conditions, endorsements and relevant disclosures. Ask the insurer or an appropriately licensed professional about anything you do not understand.

What should I do if a claim decision is unclear?

Ask the insurer for an explanation and identify the policy provisions relevant to the decision. Available complaint or appeal processes depend on the policy and jurisdiction.
